Probabilistic inference and generative modeling are central to modern machine learning. They provide tools for representing and modeling distributions, reasoning under uncertainty, prediction and decision making, with influence spanning classical probabilistic models, modern generative models, reinforcement learning, world models, and AI for science. Yet many of the most important problems remain remarkably difficult: sampling accurately from high-dimensional and multimodal distributions, performing reliable inference in complex structured models, quantifying uncertainty in large learned systems, and scaling inference to scientific and real-world problems.

At the same time, increasingly capable AI agents have achieved results on tasks that only a few years ago were viewed as requiring substantial human expertise, including software development, scientific problem solving, long-horizon research workflows, and open mathematical challenges. Can such systems help us make progress on research problems that have remained difficult for decades? Faster generation of ideas, code, and experiments does not necessarily imply better science, and as AI systems become more involved in research, the community needs clearer ways to identify genuine methodological progress, validate machine-generated results, and decide which parts of research can be accelerated.

The theme of this workshop is therefore Belief and Change. By belief, we refer to the scientific problems, principles, and research directions that the community considers important enough to pursue even when progress is slow or immediate empirical gains are limited. By change, we refer to the possibility that new computational tools, including language models and AI agents, may substantially alter both the methods we develop and the way research itself is conducted.

Mission. At a moment when both the scientific landscape and the research process are changing rapidly, we ask: what problems in probabilistic inference and generative modeling are worth sustained attention, and how should we change the way we tackle them? Through invited talks, contributed work, a panel discussion, and an experimental AI Research Track, we hope to identify important unsolved problems, examine whether new AI agents can help address them, and develop a clearer view of what reliable and meaningful progress should look like as the research process itself evolves.

Topics include but are not limited to (see Call for Papers for more details):

  • Sampling, Monte Carlo methods, sequential inference, and variational inference, particularly for high-dimensional, multimodal, or structured distributions
  • Generative modeling for graphs, 3D structures, time series, text, video, scientific data, and other structured modalities
  • Scaling and accelerating probabilistic inference and generative models, including amortized inference, learned samplers, and inference-time computation
  • Uncertainty quantification, calibration, and reliable evaluation of probabilistic models
  • Interactions between probabilistic inference and foundation models, including language models, vision-language models, world models, and reinforcement learning systems
  • Probabilistic methods for alignment, control, reward-guided generation, conditional generation, and decision making
  • Probabilistic inference and generative modeling for scientific applications, including molecular simulation, inverse problems, simulation-based inference, and physical systems
  • Applications of probabilistic methods to compression, optimization, planning, decision making, and related problems
  • Empirical and theoretical studies that expose limitations of current inference or generative modeling paradigms, including careful negative results or new evaluation methodologies

Panel: What Should Change, and What Should Stay?

In probabilistic inference and generative modeling, what can be automated, scaled, and explored, which parts of research should fundamentally change, and which principles should remain? Rather than seeking consensus, the panel brings together researchers with deliberately different views to surface the research assumptions, beliefs, and philosophies that currently shape the field.
